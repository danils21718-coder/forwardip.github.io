Codependency: signs and what to do about it

Codependency is when your entire life orbits another person and their using. You know how much they drank, where they are, what's in their pockets — and you don't know what you want. The mechanism is the same as addiction: escape from reality, obsession, denial, the illusion of control, everything organized around a single object. Theirs is a substance. Yours is a person. Which is why both of you need help, and why your part of this is real.

Important. This article is peer support and self-help, not medical care. If you are in crisis — thoughts of suicide, severe withdrawal, danger to your life — contact emergency services in your country or reach a real professional. 112 works across most of Europe; in the US call 911 or 988. Don't wait until you finish reading.

What it looks like from inside

Thoughts: I have to save him. He'll be lost without me. If I handle this correctly he'll stop. My worth is in being needed.

Feelings: constant anxiety, guilt, shame — for them and for yourself — buried anger you're not allowed to show, and fear of being alone.

Behavior: control — checking phones, counting money, tracking movements. Rescuing — clearing debts, calling in sick on their behalf, managing the relatives. Responsibility for someone else's life. Boundaries long since gone. And manipulation, because asking directly stopped working — guilt, pressure, silence.

If you recognized yourself in half of that, it isn't your personality and it isn't love. It's a described condition with a way out.

Where it comes from

Usually childhood: love given for being obedient and convenient, emotionally unavailable adults, a child assigned the rescuer role, feelings that weren't allowed to be expressed. The conclusion a child draws: to be loved, be needed and be easy.

That pattern then looks for somewhere to operate — and finds someone in endless need of saving. Not by accident.

Understanding this isn't about blaming your parents. It's about no longer treating your behavior as the only option available. It was learned, which means it can be unlearned.

Why rescuing extends the illness

Addiction progresses as long as a person can live with it and avoid consequences. Every cleared debt, every "he's sick today" to their employer, every conversation you handle for them is a bill the illness didn't have to pay.

This isn't an argument for engineering a catastrophe. It's an argument for stopping carrying what isn't yours. The distinction matters: not "I'm hurting them," but "I'm no longer doing it for them."

The position people arrive at: I can love someone without living their life. That part is theirs.

The three things to accept

You didn't cause it. You can't control it. You can't cure it.

No adult can be forced into recovery — not by pleading, threatening, paying, or loving hard enough. What you can do is stop being part of the machinery and start attending to yourself. Oddly, that's what most often shifts the situation: someone living differently right next to them does more than any amount of persuading.

How to talk so they can hear it

  1. Never while they're intoxicated. No such conversation has ever been useful. Wait for a sober morning.
  2. Talk about you, not them. Not "you got drunk again," but "I'm not sleeping and I'm frightened." Accusation triggers defense, and after that nothing gets through.
  3. Short and specific. One fact, one feeling, one request. Not ten years of evidence.
  4. No threats you won't carry out. An unenforced threat teaches them your words mean nothing.
  5. Don't shame them. Shame is fuel for using — people use to stop feeling ashamed.
  6. Offer something concrete rather than "sort yourself out": see a doctor, try a meeting, install an app.

Rebuilding boundaries

A boundary isn't a punishment or an ultimatum. It's a description of what you will do — not a demand on them.

  • I don't give money and I don't clear debts.
  • I don't lie for you to your employer or your family.
  • I don't talk to you when you're intoxicated — I'll leave and come back in the morning.
  • I don't clean up the consequences: the broken, the lost, the undone.
  • If the house becomes unsafe, I leave with the children.

One rule: if you said it, do it. A boundary broken once stops existing. And expect the first reaction to be anger — this used to work and now it doesn't. Surviving that reaction is the hardest part.

If there's any threat of violence, boundaries aren't a negotiation, they're a safety plan: where you go, who you call, where the documents are. Make it before you need it.

What to do with yourself

  1. Move the focus. Instead of "how is he?" — "what am I feeling, what do I want?" It will feel selfish at first. It isn't selfish, it's returning to yourself.
  2. Find a group for families. Al-Anon and similar are free, including online. The people there speak in your sentences, and that treats the isolation faster than anything.
  3. Take back one thing. One activity from before all this that has nothing to do with them.
  4. Practice saying no. Start with small stakes.
  5. Keep a feelings journal. Two lines each evening. In two weeks a picture appears that you can't see now.
  6. Run HALT on yourself: hungry, angry, lonely, tired. Codependent relapse into controlling happens in exactly those states.

If they don't stop

That's a possible outcome and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. But your recovery doesn't depend on theirs. People come out of codependency while living beside someone still using — and work, sleep, and have lives.

Whether to stay or leave is yours alone, and this article won't answer it. One thing is worth saying: making that decision while completely depleted is a bad idea. Recover some ground first, then decide.

The short version

Codependency isn't an exaggeration or a trend. It's a condition with the same machinery as addiction and its own way out. Rescuing extends the illness, control doesn't work, and things change when you stop living someone else's life and start attending to your own.

You didn't cause it, you can't control it, you can't cure it. You can stop being destroyed by it — and strangely, that's also the best thing you can do for them.
