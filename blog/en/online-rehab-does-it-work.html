Does online rehab work? What studies show and how to choose

"Online rehab" is a marketing phrase, not a medical one. Under it you'll find at least three very different things, and they don't deserve the same trust or the same price. Here's how to tell them apart and what the evidence actually says.

If you are in danger right now — thoughts of suicide, a seizure, confusion, chest pain, someone who won't wake up — call your emergency number: 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 112 in the EU. In the US you can also call or text 988.

Three things sold under one name

  1. Telehealth treatment from licensed clinicians. Video or phone sessions with a counselor, psychiatrist or addiction doctor — sometimes including medication for opioid or alcohol use disorder.
  2. Structured remote programs. Intensive outpatient programs delivered online: group sessions several times a week, individual counseling, check-ins.
  3. Apps and coaching. Self-guided programs, trackers, peer communities, sometimes non-clinical coaches or AI.

The first two are treatment. The third is support. All three can help, but only the first two replace anything a rehab does.

What the studies found

When addiction treatment moved online during the pandemic, researchers started comparing it with in-person care. A review published in Psychiatric Services looked at eight studies; seven found telehealth about as effective as in-person treatment for retention, satisfaction, the relationship with the therapist and substance use — though not more effective, and most studies were small.

Other work points the same way. A telemedicine opioid treatment clinic found no significant difference in retention compared with in-person delivery. A 2023 study of treatment-seeking patients found those who started with video telehealth in their first two weeks were less likely to drop out than those seen only in person. The honest summary: for many people, online treatment works roughly as well as showing up in person — and for some, removing travel and stigma helps them stay.

What online can't do

  • Medical detox from alcohol or benzodiazepines when dependence is heavy. Withdrawal from these can cause seizures and needs in-person supervision.
  • A safe environment if home is where the drinking or using happens, or isn't safe.
  • Crisis care for suicidal thoughts, psychosis or overdose.
  • Physical distance from the substance in the first weeks, which is what residential programs mostly provide.

How to choose without wasting money

  • Ask who is providing care, and check their license in your state or country.
  • Ask whether they can prescribe medication if you need it — and whether they'll refer you to in-person care if you need that.
  • Be wary of programs that promise a cure, guaranteed results, or pressure you to pay upfront for months.
  • Check whether your insurance covers it. In the US, FindTreatment.gov lists licensed programs, including ones that offer telehealth and sliding-scale fees.

The combination that tends to work

For most people, the question isn't "online or in person" but "what mix." A common pattern that clinicians describe: in-person detox if it's needed, then telehealth counseling, then peer support and a daily tool — a tracker, a journal, an app — to fill the hours between sessions. Each piece covers a gap the others leave.
