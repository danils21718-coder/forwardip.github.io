Quitting stimulants: the phases nobody warns you about

Most people who quit a stimulant expect a bad week, then normal life. When week three arrives and they still feel foggy, flat and useless, they conclude it isn't working — and go back. Knowing the actual shape of recovery prevents more relapses than willpower ever has.

If you're in real danger right now — thoughts of ending your life, chest pain, a racing heart, trouble breathing — call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK) or 112 (Europe). In the US you can also call or text 988.

There's no shame in calling. That's what they're there for.

The three phases

Acute, roughly the first three to five days. Heavy exhaustion, low mood, sugar cravings, wrecked sleep, anxiety and obsessive craving. Your body has spent its reserves of pleasure chemistry, which is exactly why everything feels empty. This part is predictable, not personal.

Subacute, week one to two. The tiredness eases but the fog stays. Forgetfulness, mood swings, a sense that your head isn't yours.

Late, week two to six. Clarity and mood start returning. Sleep is often still patchy.

What actually helps through all three: a steady seven to eight hours of sleep, real food, and daylight. Not glamorous. It's the part that works.

Then there's the long tail

After the physical withdrawal is over, your nervous system keeps recalibrating. That's post-acute withdrawal, and it's where most people get blindsided.

The symptoms come in waves — better, then worse again for no visible reason. Mental fog, forgetfulness, mood swings, anxiety, broken sleep. Stress and lack of sleep make them worse, and this can run for months.

The key thing to understand: this is neuropsychological instability, not weak character. And it's temporary. A lot of people relapse right here, because they decide it's never going to get better. It does.

Why a vape and a line belong in one article

They are not equally dangerous and I won't pretend otherwise. But addiction is a disease listed in the medical classifications, and it hits four areas at once: the body, the mind, your social life, and your sense of meaning. All four need rebuilding — not just "stop using."

That's also where the idea of powerlessness comes from in twelve-step rooms. It isn't self-pity. It's the observation that the disease doesn't respond to willpower, so fighting it alone is a losing strategy.

Cravings are a wave, not a verdict

A craving is a symptom of an illness, not a failure. And a craving isn't use: there's always a gap between the two, and the decision happens inside that gap.

The wave rises, peaks, and falls. You don't have to beat it or run from it — you can watch it and let it pass. That's urge surfing, and it works better than gritting your teeth.

Four states reliably amplify cravings: hungry, angry, lonely, tired. The shorthand is HALT. When it hits, run the checklist. Very often the problem isn't the drug at all — it's that you haven't eaten or slept.

If your use was heavy: detox first

Long use, large amounts, or mixing substances means starting with a doctor. Aim for at least three days of supervised detox so the worst part happens somewhere safe.

One hard rule: if alcohol, benzodiazepines or sleeping pills are in the mix, quitting abruptly at home can kill you. That needs medical supervision, no exceptions. Many public health services run free or low-cost detox — calling to ask commits you to nothing.

No money for rehab

Skip the fantasy of a perfect program and build the boring version.

Cut your supply lines first — the dealer's number, the group chat, the spare vape. Then fix sleep and food, because everything else gets harder without them. Then find people: Narcotics Anonymous, Cocaine Anonymous, Crystal Meth Anonymous, all free and anonymous, plenty of them online at any hour.

And keep a written record of cravings — time, place, mood, what happened that day. A sobriety tracker turns a fog into a visible pattern within a fortnight.

Relapse starts before the relapse

Using is the end of the chain, not the beginning. The chain starts earlier: denial creeps back with "I can handle a little now"; irritability builds; you quietly drop the things that were helping; you get secretive; sleep slips; self-pity accumulates.

Learn to catch it at the early stage rather than the last one, and the whole picture changes.
