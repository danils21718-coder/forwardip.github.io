Quitting stimulants: from vapes and energy drinks to coke and meth

Most people don't think of themselves as stimulant users. They just vape all day, drink three energy drinks before noon, borrow a friend's Adderall before deadlines, do a line on weekends. Then one day they notice they can't function without it. If that's where you are, this is for you — whatever you're using.

If you're in real danger right now — thoughts of ending your life, chest pain, a racing heart, trouble breathing — call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK) or 112 (Europe). In the US you can also call or text 988.

There's no shame in calling. That's what they're there for.

Why a vape and a line of coke belong in the same article

They're not equally dangerous, and I won't pretend they are. Meth and cocaine can kill you in ways a vape can't. But they run on the same trick: you borrow energy and focus from tomorrow. Tomorrow, you pay it back with a crash — flat mood, anxiety, exhaustion. And the fastest way out of the crash is another hit. That loop is what addiction recovery breaks, whether the substance is nicotine or crystal.

What stimulants actually take from you

Sleep. It goes first, and everything else follows. Short sleep makes anxiety louder and cravings stronger.

Your heart. High pulse, blood pressure spikes, palpitations. Heavy stimulants cause heart attacks and strokes in people in their twenties and thirties.

Your nerves. Constant low-grade anxiety, panic attacks, a jaw that won't unclench. A lot of people treat their "anxiety" for years without linking it to the nicotine or pills.

Your mind. Amphetamines, meth and synthetic stimulants can cause paranoia and psychosis. Sometimes it clears after stopping, sometimes it needs a psychiatrist.

Your joy. This one surprises people most. After months of artificial peaks, normal life feels grey. Food, music, people — flat. It's not who you are. It's what the drug left behind, and it recovers.

What withdrawal is really like

No honest person can give you a timeline, so I won't. But the shape is familiar. First a crash: heavy sleep, big appetite, irritability, low mood. Then cravings in waves — triggered by a place, a song, a text, money in your account. A wave builds, peaks and passes if you don't feed it. Then, often, a grey stretch where nothing feels good. That's where a lot of relapses happen. It passes too.

Cravings aren't a character flaw. They're a symptom. Treat them like one.

Heavy use? Start with supervised detox

If you've used hard stimulants for a long time, in large amounts, or mixed them with other things, see a doctor first. Aim for at least three days of supervised detox. It gets you through the worst part safely and you're not alone in it.

One warning I'll repeat everywhere: if alcohol, benzodiazepines or sleeping pills are in the mix, stopping abruptly at home can be life-threatening. That needs a medical team. Many areas offer free or low-cost detox through public health or community clinics — one phone call commits you to nothing.

For nicotine, your doctor or pharmacist can talk you through options. Medication decisions belong to them, not to the internet.

Can't afford rehab? You still have options

  1. Meetings. Narcotics Anonymous, Cocaine Anonymous, Crystal Meth Anonymous, Nicotine Anonymous, AA. Free, anonymous, in-person and online around the clock.
  2. Cut the supply line. Delete the dealer's number. Leave the group chat. Throw out the spare vape. Make relapse inconvenient.
  3. Rebuild the basics. Fixed bedtime, real meals, daylight, a walk. Boring. It's also how the brain repairs itself.
  4. Track it. A sobriety tracker and a craving log turn a fog into a pattern. After two weeks you'll see your danger hours.
  5. Tell one person. Not someone who lectures. Someone who listens.

That's online rehab in its simplest form: no magic program, just daily work plus people who understand.

The loneliness part

Addiction does its best work in isolation. Shame keeps you quiet, and quiet keeps you using. In an NA or CMA meeting nobody needs your backstory. You can sit in the back, camera off, and just listen. Lots of people say that was the first time in years they didn't feel like the only one.

If you slip

A slip isn't a reset of who you are. Look at what happened right before it, where you were alone, what you'd change — and come back. The clean days still count.
