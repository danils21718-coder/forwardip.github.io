Codependency: how to love someone with an addiction without losing yourself

You check their location. Count the bottles. Lie awake until you hear the key in the door. Call their boss to say they're sick. Pay debts you didn't make. And you're sure that if you stopped holding everything together, they'd fall apart completely. If that's your life, their addiction has become your illness too. It's called codependency — and there's a way out.

If you're in real danger right now — thoughts of ending your life, chest pain, a racing heart, trouble breathing — call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK) or 112 (Europe). In the US you can also call or text 988.

There's no shame in calling. That's what they're there for.

What codependency looks like

Your mood depends on whether they're using today. Your plans depend on whether they'll relapse. You've forgotten what you want. Codependent people usually aren't weak — they're the strong, responsible ones who carry everything until they collapse.

  • you think about them constantly;
  • you cover for them with family, work, friends;
  • you fix their problems: debts, jobs, legal trouble;
  • you swing between pity, rage and threats you don't follow through on;
  • you have your own insomnia, anxiety, tears, health problems;
  • you're ashamed and tell no one what's going on.

Why rescuing backfires

This is the hardest part to accept. Each time you pay the debt, make the excuse, clean up the mess, you take away the consequences — out of love. Without consequences, the addiction has no reason to change. It learns: "Someone will handle it."

Control doesn't work either. Pour out the bottles, hide the money, track the phone — they'll find a way, and you'll burn out.

That doesn't mean abandoning them. It means no longer doing for them what they need to do themselves.

What you can do

  1. Accept you can't cure them. Addiction is an illness. You didn't cause it and you can't fix it.
  2. Stop covering. No lies, no bailouts. Let consequences land where they belong.
  3. Talk when they're sober. Calmly, about yourself: "I'm scared when you come home high," not "You're a mess again."
  4. Set boundaries you'll actually keep. "If you drink at home, I'll stay at my sister's." Only say what you'll do.
  5. Offer help, don't force it. Share where they can get help when ready. Recovery has to be their choice.
  6. Take your life back. Sleep, friends, work, things you used to love. That's not selfish. It's survival.

You need support too

Al-Anon is for families and friends of people who drink. Nar-Anon is for families of people who use drugs. Co-Dependents Anonymous (CoDA) is for anyone who recognises the pattern in themselves. All free and anonymous, with online meetings.

Many people walk in asking "How do I save my partner?" and walk out understanding they have to start with themselves — surrounded by people living the same thing.

If there's violence

If you or your children aren't safe, safety comes first. Call the police, go to family, contact a local domestic violence service. Love doesn't mean enduring abuse.

Bottom line

Codependency isn't love — it's an illness that grows alongside addiction. You can't cure the person you love, but you can stop shielding them from consequences, set boundaries and get your own support.
