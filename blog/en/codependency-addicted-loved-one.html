Codependency: why the harder you rescue, the worse it gets

There's a formula that makes this easy to recognise: "if they're okay, then I'm okay." Your mood today depends on whether they used. Your plans depend on whether they'll relapse. What you want is no longer information you have.

That's codependency — involvement in someone else's life so total that you lose contact with your own feelings, boundaries and needs. It isn't love. Love respects boundaries. This is fear, control and dissolving into another person.

If you're in real danger right now — thoughts of ending your life, chest pain, a racing heart, trouble breathing — call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK) or 112 (Europe). In the US you can also call or text 988.

There's no shame in calling. That's what they're there for.

Who ends up here

Not weak people. Usually the strong, responsible ones who carry everything. The recognisable thoughts: "I have to save him," "without me he'd be finished," "my value is being needed." Then insomnia, blood pressure, crying, shame, and telling nobody what happens at home.

Why rescue works against both of you

Every time you pay the debt, phone their boss, invent the story for relatives — you remove the consequences, out of love. Without consequences the illness has no reason to change. It concludes: someone will handle it.

Control fails too. Pour it away, hide the money, check the phone — they'll find a route, and you'll burn out. The line between care and rescue is simple: care is what you do for yourself and the relationship; rescue is what you do instead of them.

Which part of you is speaking

Useful frame. In any conversation a person occupies one of three states.

Critical Parent — "you should," "you're drunk again," "look what you've done to this family." Judgement and demands.

Child — hurt, tears, fear, threats to leave that never happen.

Adult — a sober look at the facts, calm statements about yourself.

Almost every conversation with an addicted person runs from the first two and ends identically: a row, a slammed door, nothing changed. And they respond in kind, as a defensive child or a snarling parent.

Next time, mid-argument, check who's talking in you. If it's the critic or the hurt child, the conversation is already useless — postpone it. Talk from the adult, and only when they're sober.

In practice it's the difference between "you drank the money again, you don't care about this family" and "I don't sleep and I'm frightened when you don't answer your phone." The first invites defence. The second is a fact about you, and there's nothing to argue with.

What you can do

Accept you can't cure them. Addiction is an illness; you're neither its cause nor its cure.

Stop covering. No lies on their behalf, no paying debts, no smoothing things over.

Set boundaries you will actually keep. "If you drink at home, I'll stay at my sister's" — only if you'll really go. An unkept boundary teaches them your words mean nothing.

Offer help rather than force it. Tell them where to go when they're ready. The decision stays theirs, which is the hard part.

Taking your life back

This isn't selfishness or giving up on them. A burnt-out person helps nobody.

Start small and finish it — one completed small thing rebuilds self-trust better than grand plans. Sleep at a normal hour. See the doctor about the thing you've postponed for six months. One evening a week that belongs to you.

And self-love here isn't indulgence or excuses. It's care, respect, honesty and responsibility towards yourself — usually as broken in the family as it is in the person using.

Where to go

Al-Anon for families of people who drink. Nar-Anon for families of people using drugs. CoDA for anyone recognising this pattern in themselves. Free, anonymous, online meetings available.

Most people arrive asking how to save someone, and stay because it's the first time in years they're among people living exactly the same thing.

If there's danger

If you or the children aren't safe, safety outranks everything. Police, family, a crisis service. Love does not require tolerating violence.
