Cravings: how they work and what to do right now

A craving is a wave. It rises, peaks, and falls, and every single one passes on its own if you don't act on it. You don't have to defeat it — you have to outlast it. That sounds too simple for the state you're probably in right now, and it's what every working technique is built on: not fighting, just staying put until the wave goes down. Here's what to do in the next ten minutes, and then how the thing actually works.

Important. This article is peer support and self-help, not medical care. If you are in crisis — thoughts of suicide, severe withdrawal, danger to your life — contact emergency services in your country or reach a real professional. 112 works across most of Europe; in the US call 911 or 988. Don't wait until you finish reading.

If it's hitting right now

  1. Leave. If there's a trigger nearby — a place, a person, a situation — go, without explaining or apologizing.
  2. Set a ten-minute timer. Don't promise yourself "never." Ten minutes. Then another ten.
  3. Say it to a living person: "I'm craving right now." Call one of your three numbers. Saying it out loud is strength, not weakness.
  4. Run HALT: am I hungry, angry, lonely, tired?
  5. Use your body. Fast walk outside, cold-free shower, twenty squats, clean something. Don't sit still.
  6. Ask one question: what can I do right now so I don't use? Short answer, then do it.

Now the reasons that works.

What a craving actually is

It's intense, unpleasant pressure carrying a demand for immediate relief. It's a symptom of the illness — like a cough with a cold — not evidence that your recovery is going wrong.

Real cravings appear at least a couple of days into abstinence. Anything before that is still withdrawal, which is a different state. They can show up after days or after months, and the months version frightens people most.

Three rules worth learning by heart:

  1. A craving is not a relapse. Feeling it and doing it are separate events.
  2. Cravings are ripples in water: the further from the last use, the weaker and rarer they get.
  3. Cravings are waves: rise, peak, fall. Each one ends.

Two kinds, and don't confuse them

Trigger cravings. Fired by association: a place, a person, a smell, a time of day, money in your account, a specific song. They run on memory of the good part. The treatment is avoidance — remove the triggers from your life.

Stress cravings. Fired by hard emotions: a fight, exhaustion, shame, boredom, fear. The substance was the way to mute pain, and your brain re-offers it. The treatment is learning to sit with states — conversation, journaling, routine.

When both land together — a brutal day plus running into someone from the old crowd — risk peaks. Which is why the strategy is always double: remove triggers and learn to handle stress. One alone isn't enough. Cut the people out but never learn to survive a bad evening, and you'll go down on the first bad evening.

Urge surfing

The name carries the technique. An experienced surfer neither fights the wave nor runs from it — they balance until it flattens.

In practice: notice it and name it — "this is a craving." Don't panic. Don't make sudden moves. Observe it like weather: where it sits in your body, whether it's still climbing or already easing. And repeat one thing to yourself: steady, this passes.

The peak usually lasts minutes, not hours. It feels like forever. By the clock, it isn't.

HALT: four questions that clear half of it

Four states where you're most exposed: hungry, angry, lonely, tired. Check them every evening, not only when things are bad.

  • Hungry — eat. An actual meal, not sugar. Solved in half an hour.
  • Angry — put it into words or onto paper. Neither swallow it nor dump it on someone.
  • Lonely — call someone. This state makes you want to shut down; do the opposite.
  • Tired — sleep. Sleep debt drives cravings harder than anything else.

A large share of what feels like an unbeatable craving is hunger and exhaustion. Free to test, takes an hour.

Working with the thoughts

Ordering yourself not to think about using doesn't work — you think about it more. Substitution works. Not "how good it started," but "how it ended." Not how you got in, but how you got out.

Write two lists by hand in advance: the benefits of not using, the costs of using. Specific, your own words, your own life. Carry it and read it during a craving. In an acute state you can't compose that; you can read it.

It's also worth challenging the statements themselves: "I can't have one and stop," "I don't need it to relax." Don't argue with the feeling — test the claim.

How not to handle a craving

  • Pretending it isn't there.
  • Sitting alone and waiting it out.
  • Replaying the good parts.
  • Burying yourself in work — that's a delay, not a solution.
  • Taking the pressure out on people close to you.
  • Caffeine and cigarettes as a substitute.
  • Shutting everyone out.

One more: methods based on pain or sharp physical shock get recommended a lot and are excluded here. For anyone with a history of self-harm they lead straight back, and serious programs don't use them.

Myths that get in the way

"Wanting it means recovery isn't working." False. Cravings are normal and universal.

"Wanting it means I'm weak." False. It's a symptom, not a character trait.

"Months in, there shouldn't be cravings." False. They can arrive later, and that isn't a reversal.

"Better not to admit it." The dangerous one. A craving spoken out loud weakens; a hidden one grows.

Build the plan in advance

  1. List your triggers — places, people, situations, states. Put it somewhere visible.
  2. Three phone numbers you can use at any hour.
  3. Two or three actions that reliably shift you: outside, shower, call, gym.
  4. A rule for unavoidable events like weddings: go with someone who knows, leave at the first signal.
  5. Tell the people close to you what your triggers are, before you need them to know.

The short version

Cravings don't last and they don't have to end in using. They have a shape, two causes, and predictable behavior. The task isn't heroic resistance — it's ten minutes, then another ten, without being alone and in silence while you do it.

The further you get from the last time, the weaker and rarer they become. That happens on its own if you let time pass. Only today.
