Video game addiction: how to tell if you're hooked and what to do about it

Games aren't the enemy. Most people who play a lot are fine. But for some, gaming quietly takes over sleep, work, school, and relationships, and "I'll stop after this match" stops being true. This guide helps you figure out which group you're in, and if it's the second one, how to take back control without white-knuckling it.

Hours aren't the test. Control and consequences are.

The World Health Organization recognizes "gaming disorder" in its diagnostic manual (ICD-11). The key features are about control and harm, not screen time:

  • Impaired control: you play longer or more often than you intended, and can't cut back when you try.
  • Priority: gaming wins out over other interests, responsibilities, sleep, and people.
  • Continuing despite harm: grades slip, work suffers, relationships strain, and you keep going anyway.

Typically, this pattern lasts at least 12 months before a diagnosis. A quick self-check: if gaming were taken away for a week, would you feel mildly bored, or genuinely panicked and empty? The second answer is worth paying attention to.

What gaming might be doing for you

Problem gaming is rarely about the game itself. It's usually solving another problem, at least temporarily:

  • Anxiety and stress. A game is predictable and controllable in a way life isn't.
  • Loneliness. Your squad or Discord might be your main social life.
  • Feeling stuck. Ranking up gives a sense of progress that a dead-end job or stalled degree doesn't.
  • Depression or ADHD. Games deliver constant stimulation and rewards that a struggling brain craves.

Name the job gaming does for you. If you remove it without replacing that job, you'll likely drift back.

The design isn't neutral

Many modern games are built to maximize engagement: daily login rewards, streaks, battle passes that expire, limited-time events, ranked ladders that reset, and loot boxes that work a lot like slot machines. None of this means you're weak. It means you're up against teams of designers whose job is to keep you playing. Knowing which hooks get you helps you avoid them.

A 30-day reset plan

Trying to "play less" while the games are installed and your friends are online rarely works at first. A full 30-day break gives your routine and your reward system a chance to recalibrate.

  1. Tell people. Let your gaming friends know you're taking a month off so they don't keep inviting you. Tell someone offline, too, so you're accountable.
  2. Remove access. Uninstall games and launchers, log out of accounts, and put consoles out of sight or with a friend. Use your device's built-in screen time controls and let someone else set the passcode.
  3. Plan your evenings in advance. Unstructured time is where relapses happen. Put specific things on your calendar: gym, a class, cooking, a walk, calling a friend.
  4. Fix your sleep. Set a consistent bedtime and keep devices out of the bedroom. Better sleep alone reduces cravings and irritability.
  5. Move your body. Even 20–30 minutes of exercise a day gives your brain a quick reward and helps with the restlessness of the first couple of weeks.
  6. Find a team outside the game. A rec league, climbing gym, volunteering, a board game night. It's harder than queuing up for a match, but it rebuilds the social life gaming was standing in for.

Expect the first 1–2 weeks to feel boring and irritable. That's normal and usually eases.

Going back: moderation or quitting for good?

After a reset, some people can play in moderation; others can't. If you want to try, wait at least a month, and set firm rules: single-player games with an ending, no ranked modes, no loot boxes or battle passes, gaming only on set days, with a timer someone else controls. If the rules start slipping, that's your answer.

If you're spending money you can't afford on in-game purchases, read our guide on gambling addiction. Loot box spending can follow the same pattern.

For parents of a teen who games too much

Taking the console away in anger usually backfires: it turns gaming into a power struggle and a symbol of independence. What tends to work better:

  • Get curious first. Ask what they play, who with, and what they like about it. Watch them play. You'll learn what the game gives them.
  • Make a family media plan together. Agree on limits, when gaming happens (after homework, not after bedtime), and consequences for breaking the rules. Write it down.
  • Protect sleep above everything. Devices charge outside the bedroom overnight. This one rule does a lot of the heavy lifting.
  • Look for the underlying issue. Bullying, social anxiety, a family upheaval, or untreated ADHD or depression often drive heavy gaming.

If your teen is refusing school, barely sleeping, isolating for weeks, or talking about not wanting to live, get help from a pediatrician, therapist, or psychiatrist. That's beyond a household rules problem.

When to get professional help

Consider a therapist if you've tried breaks a few times and keep falling back, if gaming has already cost you a job, grades, or a relationship, or if depression, anxiety, or ADHD may be underneath. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has the strongest evidence for problem gaming. Mention any other habits too, like gambling, drinking, or stimulant use.

The short version

Gaming becomes a problem when you lose control and it causes real harm, not because of the hours. Figure out what gaming does for you, take a 30-day break with your evenings planned, fix your sleep, and build a life offline that fills the same needs.
