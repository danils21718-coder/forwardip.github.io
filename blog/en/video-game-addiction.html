Video game addiction: why confiscating the console backfires

The most common parental mistake is starting at the last step. Take the console, cut the wifi, remove the PC. Then wonder why it got worse: shouting, lying, secret sessions on a phone at 3 a.m.

The reason is that people don't change all at once. They move through stages, and tools from one stage are useless at another.

If you're in real danger right now — thoughts of ending your life, chest pain, a racing heart, trouble breathing — call 911 (US, Canada), 999 (UK) or 112 (Europe). In the US you can also call or text 988.

There's no shame in calling. That's what they're there for.

Five stages worth knowing

Precontemplation. The person doesn't see a problem, or isn't thinking about changing. Roughly half deny there's an issue at all.

Contemplation. Problem acknowledged, weighing it up.

Preparation. Decided, getting ready.

Action. Changing the behaviour.

Maintenance. Holding the change. And there's a sixth stage everyone forgets: relapse.

Why it matters: handing someone action-stage tools while they're in precontemplation produces a war, not a change. A confiscated console is an action-stage tool.

The reverse applies too. Beating yourself up for "thinking about quitting for six months and doing nothing" misreads the map. Contemplation isn't laziness, it's a required stage.

Where the line is

A hobby is something you choose. An addiction chooses for you. Honest checklist: you plan to stop and don't; without it you're irritable or empty; you lie about the hours; you dropped things you used to like; sleep, school or work are suffering and that doesn't stop you; money goes on the game that you need elsewhere.

One item means nothing. Several, for months, is a conversation.

What the game is actually giving

Modern games are built to hold you: daily rewards, streaks, loot boxes, ranked ladders, a squad expecting you. Ordinary life can't match that reward rate.

But look underneath the behaviour at the need. Humans need belonging, safety, recognition, love. Those needs are legitimate. What goes wrong is the method used to meet them. In the game you're competent, needed by your team, and your progress is visible. Remove the game and the needs remain with nothing to meet them.

That's the whole reason confiscation fails. What works is giving the need another outlet.

If this is you

Start with a week of observation. Log not just hours but your state before you start. Bored? Anxious? After an argument? Empty? A week later you won't have "I play too much," you'll have a specific pattern.

Then add friction. Uninstall from the phone, log out, remove the saved card, move the console out of the bedroom. Every extra step between impulse and launch is a second bought for thinking.

And decide what fills the hole. Remove the game and leave nothing, and you get empty time — which is fuel for cravings. Take one small goal and finish it; a completed small thing rebuilds self-trust far better than "change my whole life starting Monday."

If it's your child

First, work out the stage. If they don't consider it a problem, demanding a plan is pointless. What works there is a conversation with no pressure: what does the game give you, what's good about it. Listening without eye-rolling.

When "yeah, maybe I do lose whole nights" appears, that's contemplation, and now you can discuss what they want.

Set rules together. A rule imposed from above reads as occupied territory, and the response is guerrilla warfare.

And look at the rest of their life: bullying, isolation, pressure, what's happening at home. The game is usually shelter, not cause.

Support

ITAA — Internet and Technology Addicts Anonymous — runs free online twelve-step meetings. If gaming is covering anxiety or depression, add a therapist who understands addiction.
