Craving: what to do in the first twenty minutes

A craving is not a command. It feels like one — that is the whole problem. It arrives with total certainty, as if the decision has already been made somewhere above your head and you are just being informed. But it behaves like a wave. It rises, it peaks, it falls. The peak rarely lasts longer than fifteen or twenty minutes. Your job is not to win an argument with it. Your job is to still be here when it drops.

If this is more than a craving

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, or if withdrawal has brought seizures, confusion, or hallucinations — that is a medical emergency, not a willpower problem. Call your local emergency number now.

US and Canada: 988. UK and Ireland: 116 123. Most of Europe: 112.

Why fighting it head-on does not work

If you try to beat a craving by force, you end up in a wrestling match with your own head. Every "no" needs a "yes" to push against, so you keep the thing alive by arguing with it. People who fight hardest often lose fastest, because fighting is exhausting and you cannot stay in that posture for long.

The alternative is not surrender. It is refusing to treat the craving as an emergency that requires an immediate answer. It showed up. Fine. It will also leave.

What is actually underneath

Here is the part most people miss. A craving is almost never just a craving. It is a bundle of feelings that has no other language.

Underneath, you will usually find one of a handful of things. Anxiety about something you have not dealt with. Anger you swallowed earlier today. Loneliness you have been calling "being tired." Boredom, which sounds trivial and is not — empty time is fuel. Sometimes plain nostalgia, a memory of a version of yourself you miss.

If you can name what is underneath, the craving loses most of its authority. It stops being a mysterious force and becomes a signal about something specific that you can actually address.

The first twenty minutes, step by step

  1. Say it out loud, even alone. "I want to use right now." Naming it moves it from the driver's seat to the passenger seat. Unspoken things run the show.
  2. Run HALT. Hungry, angry, lonely, tired. If any of them is true, that is your real problem. Eat something. Say the angry thing to someone. Call a person. Sleep. Arguing with a craving at one in the morning on an empty stomach is a fight you set up to lose.
  3. Find it in your body. Where is it, actually? Chest, throat, stomach, hands? Bodies signal before minds do — clenched jaw, hot face, sweating palms, a lump in the throat. Locating it turns an overwhelming feeling into an observable one.
  4. Breathe with a long exit. In for four, out for eight. The long out-breath is what does the work. Do it for two minutes, not two breaths.
  5. Set a timer for twenty minutes. You are not deciding anything today. You are deciding nothing for twenty minutes. That is all you have committed to.
  6. Move. Ten or fifteen minutes of anything physical burns off the adrenaline. Walk, push-ups, stairs, cleaning something. It does not have to be a workout.
  7. Tell one person. This is the step people skip, and it is the one that matters most. A sponsor, a friend, a group chat, a mentor, a notebook. A craving you have told someone about is a different size than one you have kept.

Two columns, done by hand

If the wave is still up after all that, there is one more thing worth doing, and it works better on paper than in your head.

Draw a line down a page. On one side, write what actually happens if you use. Not the first ten minutes — the next morning, the next week, the conversation you will have to have, the counter going back to zero. On the other side, what happens if you do not.

Your head is very good at showing you a highlight reel and skipping the rest. Writing forces the rest back into frame.

What not to reach for

Some advice going around involves shocking your body — ice, snapping a rubber band, cold water. Skip it. For anyone with any history of self-harm, that is not a coping tool, it is the same behaviour wearing a different coat. There are plenty of ways to get through twenty minutes that do not involve hurting yourself.

After the wave passes

When it drops, do not just move on relieved. Write down what happened, while it is fresh. What was going on right before it hit. What feelings were in there. What you did. What actually helped.

Do that ten times and you will have something more valuable than any article: a map of your own triggers, in your own handwriting. You will start seeing the wave forming before it arrives.

The short version

Cravings are not commands and they are not permanent. They peak and they fall. Name it, check HALT, find it in your body, breathe out slowly, wait twenty minutes, move your body, and tell someone. The last one is where the chain usually breaks — not because talking is magic, but because a feeling that stays inside keeps its power, and one that gets said out loud does not.
