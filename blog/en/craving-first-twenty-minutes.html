A craving just hit. Here's what to do for the next twenty minutes

Don't decide anything yet. You don't have to win against this craving forever — just for the next twenty minutes. That's a realistic job, and this page walks you through it.

If you are in danger right now — thoughts of suicide, a seizure, confusion, chest pain, someone who won't wake up — call your emergency number: 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 112 in the EU. In the US you can also call or text 988.

Why twenty minutes

The psychologist Alan Marlatt, who built much of modern relapse prevention, noticed that cravings behave like waves. Left alone — not fed, not fought — they climb, crest and fall. Clinicians who teach his method usually put the whole wave at around 15 to 30 minutes. From the inside it feels like it will only get worse. It won't. It gets worse until it peaks, and then it starts to drop.

Minutes 0–2: create distance

  • Get physically away from the thing — leave the room, the store, the kitchen.
  • Put the phone with the dealer's number or the betting app in another room.
  • Say it out loud: "I'm having a craving." Not "I want a drink." Naming it as an event puts a small gap between you and it.

Minutes 2–5: check the four basics

Recovery groups have used the same quick check for decades. Are you:

  • Hungry?
  • Angry or resentful?
  • Lonely?
  • Tired?

A surprising number of cravings are one of these wearing a disguise. If you're hungry, eat something now. If you're lonely, the next step matters most.

Minutes 5–15: surf it

  1. Find where the craving lives in your body — chest, throat, hands, stomach.
  2. Rate it 0 to 10.
  3. Breathe slowly. Out longer than in.
  4. Watch the sensation change. It will — tighter, looser, moving around.
  5. Re-rate every few minutes. Write the numbers down if you can.

What does the research say? In a 2009 study, smokers given brief urge-surfing instructions didn't feel weaker cravings in the moment — but they smoked fewer cigarettes over the following week. A later trial of the full mindfulness-based relapse prevention program found less drug use and heavy drinking a year out than standard care. The technique doesn't make the urge disappear. It changes what you do with it.

Minutes 15–20: reach a human

Text or call someone. It doesn't have to be about the craving; it can just be "hey, rough night." Most people who relapse say they were alone when it happened. Breaking that isolation is often what actually tips the wave down.

If there's no one: in the US, the SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-4357) is free, confidential and open 24/7. In the UK, Alcoholics Anonymous runs a national helpline, and most countries have online AA and NA meetings running around the clock.

After it passes

Take two minutes and write down what happened: time, place, what you were feeling just before, what helped. This isn't homework for its own sake. After ten of these, you'll see your own pattern — Friday after work, after calls with your mother, when you skip dinner — and you can plan for it instead of getting ambushed.

Two kinds of craving. One is triggered by a cue — a bar sign, payday, a song — and fades fast. The other builds over days from stress, grief or emptiness. The first one, you surf. The second one is telling you something about your life that needs more than twenty minutes: a conversation, a meeting, a change.

Every wave you ride out without using teaches your brain that the craving isn't an order. The next one usually hits a little softer.
