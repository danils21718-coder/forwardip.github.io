Does a sobriety tracker actually help? What to log besides days

A number on a screen that goes up by one every morning sounds too simple to matter. But tracking is one of the few app features that has been tested in randomized trials — and the results suggest it's not just a gimmick.

If you are in danger right now — thoughts of suicide, a seizure, confusion, chest pain, someone who won't wake up — call your emergency number: 911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 112 in the EU. In the US you can also call or text 988.

What the evidence says

Researchers at University College London have spent years testing an alcohol-reduction app called Drink Less. In a large randomized trial, people who were recommended the app cut their weekly drinking by about two units more after six months than people given standard online advice (the size of the effect depended on how missing data were handled).

The more interesting finding came when they asked why. Two things partly explained the effect: whether people kept using the app, and whether they used its tracking feature. An earlier trial that tested the app's components separately pointed the same way — enhanced self-monitoring and feedback was among the parts linked to better outcomes.

These studies were about cutting down, not about full sobriety, and they relied on people reporting their own drinking. But the mechanism — noticing and recording — is the same one a sobriety tracker uses.

Why counting works

Memory is a bad witness in addiction. The rough mornings fade fast; the good nights stay vivid. A record doesn't edit. It also turns an abstract decision ("I'm not drinking") into something you can see grow — and something you'd have to actively break.

What to track besides days

The day count is the least informative number. The useful data is around it:

  • Cravings: time, strength (0–10), where you were, what you were feeling, what you did.
  • Mood and sleep: a quick daily score. Bad sleep often shows up a few days before a rough patch.
  • Connection: did you talk to anyone in recovery today?
  • What helped: the walk, the call, the meeting — so you can do it on purpose next time.

After two or three weeks, patterns show up that you'd never notice in the moment: cravings cluster on Sunday evenings, or after calls with one person, or whenever you skip lunch. That's the real payoff.

Paper or app?

Whichever you'll actually use every day. Apps are easier to keep on you and can show trends; paper feels more deliberate and some people trust it more. The tool matters far less than the habit of opening it.

The reset problem

A big counter is motivating until it goes back to zero. Then it can turn into "all that time wasted," and that thought drives the next drink more than the slip itself. Two things help:

  • keep a separate count of total sober days, which never resets;
  • treat a slip as a data point: log what led up to it, the same way you log cravings.

What a tracker won't do

It won't replace people. It won't handle a medical withdrawal. It won't fix the reasons you drank or used. It's a mirror — honest and useful, but only as good as what you do with what you see in it.
